# Denominations, Events, Terms

## B. Denominations

Chapell: *"What are the historical origins and distinctives of the following?"*

| Tier | Denomination | Origins | Distinctives |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Presbyterian** | From the **Swiss and Scottish Reformation**: Calvin's Geneva; **John Knox** brought it to Scotland (the Church of Scotland, 1560); the **Westminster Assembly** (1643-49) produced its standards. The first presbytery in America was organized in **1706** (Francis Makemie). | **Reformed theology** (Calvinism) in the Westminster Standards; government by **elders** in **courts** (session, presbytery, General Assembly); covenant theology and **infant baptism**; the regulative principle in worship. |
| 1 | **Lutheran** | From **Martin Luther's** protest (the 95 Theses, **1517**); the **Augsburg Confession** (1530) and **Book of Concord** (1580). | Justification by faith alone; the **law-gospel** distinction; the **real presence** of Christ's body and blood "in, with, and under" the elements; infant baptism; a more liturgical worship, with fewer changes than the Reformed made to medieval worship; the **two kingdoms**. |
| 2 | **Baptist** | Grew out of English **Separatists** in the early 1600s (**John Smyth** and **Thomas Helwys**, 1609) and the **Particular Baptists** (1630s-40s, the **London Confession** of 1644); **Roger Williams** founded the first Baptist church in America (Providence, 1639). The **Southern Baptist Convention** was founded in **1845**. | **Believer's baptism** (usually by **immersion**), rejecting infant baptism; a **regenerate church membership**; **congregational government** and autonomy of the local church; **religious liberty** and separation of church and state; most are evangelical, many Calvinistic (Particular) or Arminian (General). |
| 2 | **Methodist** | **John and Charles Wesley**, Anglican priests, led a renewal in 18th-century England (the "Holy Club" at Oxford, 1729; Wesley's **Aldersgate** experience, **1738**), with **George Whitefield** and **field preaching**. The **Methodist Episcopal Church** was organized in America in **1784**. | **Arminian** theology; emphasis on **assurance, holiness, and Christian perfection**; **itinerant preachers**, **class meetings** and a **connectional** system with **bishops**; hymn-singing (Charles Wesley). |
| 2 | **Episcopal** (and Anglican) | The **Church of England** broke with Rome under **Henry VIII** (the **Act of Supremacy**, **1534**); **Cranmer's Book of Common Prayer** (1549, 1552); the **Thirty-Nine Articles** (1563) under Elizabeth. The **Episcopal Church** in America was organized after the Revolution (**1789**). | **Episcopal government** (bishops in apostolic succession); a **liturgy** set out in the **Book of Common Prayer**; the *via media* between Rome and the Reformation (Protestant doctrine, Catholic order); a broad range of views (evangelical, Anglo-Catholic, liberal). |
| 2 | **Mennonite** (Anabaptist) | From the **Radical Reformation**: the **Swiss Brethren** in **Zurich** (**1525**, Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz); the **Schleitheim Confession** (1527); named for the Dutch leader **Menno Simons** (1496-1561). | **Believer's baptism**; a **gathered**, **disciplined** church separate from the state and the world; **pacifism**; refusal of **oaths**; **simple living**; congregational structure. |
| 2 | **Pentecostal** | From the **Holiness** movement and revivals around **1900**: **Charles Parham** (Topeka, **1901**) and the **Azusa Street revival** in Los Angeles (**1906**, led by **William Seymour**). The **Assemblies of God** formed in **1914**. | **Baptism in the Holy Spirit** as a distinct experience, often evidenced by **speaking in tongues**; the continuing **sign gifts**, healing, and prophecy; a spontaneous and experiential worship; strong emphasis on **evangelism and missions**. |
| 2 | **Orthodox** (Eastern Orthodox) | The Eastern half of the early church, centered in **Constantinople**; the **Great Schism of 1054** from Rome (over the **filioque** clause in the Creed and the **papal primacy**); strong in Greece, the Balkans, Russia (from **988**). | Acceptance of the **seven ecumenical councils**; **tradition** alongside Scripture; **liturgy** and **icons**; **theosis** (deification) as the goal of salvation; **autocephalous** national churches under **patriarchs**; **no papal supremacy**; the Trinity without the *filioque*. |

## C. Events

Chapell: *"Briefly identify and give dates for the following."*

| Tier | Event | Date | Significance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Council of Nicea** | **325** | The first ecumenical council, called by **Constantine**; condemned **Arius** and affirmed that the Son is **of the same substance (*homoousios*)** with the Father; produced the **Nicene Creed** (completed at Constantinople, 381). |
| 1 | **Council of Chalcedon** | **451** | Defined Christ as **one person in two natures**, truly God and truly man, "without confusion, change, division, or separation"; condemned **Eutyches** (monophysitism) and **Nestorius**. |
| 1 | **Reformation** | **1517** (Luther's 95 Theses) to about **1648** | The 16th-century movement to reform the church by **Scripture and the gospel of grace**: Luther, Zwingli, Calvin; split Western Christendom into Protestant and Roman Catholic churches. |
| 2 | **Counter-Reformation** (Catholic Reformation) | **1545-63** (Council of Trent), 1540 (Jesuits) | Rome's response: the **Council of Trent** reaffirmed tradition, the seven sacraments, justification by faith and works, and condemned Protestant teaching; founding of the **Jesuits** (Ignatius Loyola, 1540); reform of abuses; renewed missions. |
| 2 | **Heidelberg Catechism** | **1563** | The Reformed catechism written by **Zacharias Ursinus** and **Caspar Olevianus** for the German Palatinate, at the request of Elector **Frederick III**; 129 questions in 52 Lord's Days; famous for its first question ("My only comfort in life and in death...") and its structure of **guilt, grace, gratitude**. |
| 2 | **Belgic Confession** | **1561** | Written by **Guido de Brès** for the Reformed churches of the Netherlands and Belgium, under persecution; 37 articles. With the Heidelberg Catechism and the Canons of Dort, it forms the **Three Forms of Unity**. |
| 1 | **Synod of Dort** | **1618-19** | The Reformed synod in the Netherlands that answered the **Remonstrants** (followers of **Arminius**); produced the **Canons of Dort**, the source of the **five points** of Calvinism (TULIP). |
| 1 | **Westminster Assembly** | **1643-49** | Convened by the English Parliament at **Westminster Abbey** to reform the Church of England; produced the **Westminster Confession of Faith** (1646), the **Larger and Shorter Catechisms** (1647), and the Form of Government and Directory for Worship; the doctrinal standards of the Presbyterian churches. |
| 2 | **Pietism** | Late 17th to 18th century (**Spener**'s *Pia Desideria*, **1675**) | A renewal movement in German Lutheranism stressing **personal faith, Bible study, small groups, holy living**, and missions (Spener, **Francke** at **Halle**; **Zinzendorf** and the **Moravians**); it influenced Wesley and the awakenings. |
| 1 | **Great Awakening(s)** | **First: 1730s-40s**; **Second: c. 1790-1840** | **First:** revival in the colonies, with **Jonathan Edwards** (Northampton, 1734-35), **George Whitefield** (1739-40), and the **Tennents**. **Second:** revivals on the frontier (**Cane Ridge**, 1801) and in the East, **Charles Finney**; led to mission and reform societies. |
| 2 | **Old School/New School** | **1837** | The **division of the Presbyterian Church in the USA** over theology (the "New Haven" theology and revivalism), **church polity** (the Plan of Union and voluntary societies versus denominational boards), and slavery; **Old School** (Princeton, **Charles Hodge**) and **New School** (more revivalist, cooperative with Congregationalists). The North's branches **reunited in 1869**. |
| 2 | **Auburn Affirmation** | **1923** (published **1924**) | A declaration signed by about **1,274** Presbyterian ministers (PCUSA) protesting the **General Assembly's** requirement of the **"five fundamentals"** (1923) and defending **doctrinal liberty**; seen as a victory for **modernism** in the northern church and a key moment in the **fundamentalist-modernist** controversy. |

## D. Definitions

Chapell: *"Briefly define."*

| Tier | Term | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | **Scholasticism** | The **medieval method of theology** in the universities, using **logic and dialectic** to organize and defend doctrine (**Anselm**, **Peter Lombard**, **Aquinas**); a **Protestant scholasticism** (Turretin, Owen, the Westminster divines) later used the same precision. |
| 2 | **Babylonian Captivity** | In church history, the **papacy at Avignon** in France (**1309-77**), when the popes lived under French influence. (Luther's **"Babylonian Captivity of the Church"**, 1520, is a treatise against Rome's sacramental system.) |
| 2 | **Humanism** | The **Renaissance** movement ("to the sources," *ad fontes*) that studied the classics and the Bible in the original languages (**Erasmus**, whose Greek New Testament of **1516** helped the Reformation). Christian humanism differs from modern **secular** humanism, which makes man the measure. |
| 3 | **Radical Reformation** | The wing of the Reformation that went **beyond Luther and Calvin**: **Anabaptists** (believer's baptism, a gathered church, separation of church and state), **spiritualists**, and **rationalists**; persecuted by Catholics and Protestants alike. |
| 2 | **Puritanism** | The English movement (16th-17th centuries) seeking to **purify** the Church of England of remaining Roman elements, and to promote **Calvinist doctrine, preaching, holy living**, and a **Sabbath**; included **Owen, Baxter, Bunyan, Goodwin**, and the Westminster divines; influenced New England. |
| 2 | **Modernism** | **Liberal theology** that adapted Christianity to modern thought (**Schleiermacher**, **Harnack**): it questioned the supernatural, biblical inerrancy, the virgin birth, the atonement, and the resurrection, and emphasized **experience and ethics**; the target of **fundamentalism**. |
| 2 | **Fundamentalism** | An early 20th-century movement defending the "**fundamentals**" of the faith against modernism (published in *The Fundamentals*, 1910-15): the **inerrancy** of Scripture, the **virgin birth**, **substitutionary atonement**, **bodily resurrection**, and the **reality of miracles** (the PCUSA's "five points"). Later also marked by **separatism** and cultural withdrawal. |
| 2 | **Neo-orthodoxy** | A 20th-century movement (**Karl Barth**, **Emil Brunner**, **Reinhold Niebuhr**) reacting to liberalism, recovering themes such as **sin, grace, and the Word of God**, but treating Scripture as a **witness** to revelation rather than revelation itself, and often denying **inerrancy**. |
